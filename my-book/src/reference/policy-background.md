# Policy and Standards Background

This page gives learners and instructors the legal and professional context behind the work. It is a summary for orientation and **not legal advice**. For decisions that depend on the law, consult your agency or state department of education.

## Why NIMAS exists

The **National Instructional Materials Accessibility Standard (NIMAS)** is part of the Individuals with Disabilities Education Act (IDEA). It was added by the 2004 reauthorization to speed up delivery of accessible textbooks to students who are blind or have other print disabilities.

Key facts, with their sources in the regulations:

- States must adopt NIMAS. The requirement is at **20 U.S.C. §1412(a)(23)** and **34 CFR §300.172** (access to instructional materials).
- States may coordinate with the **National Instructional Materials Access Center (NIMAC)**, a national repository of publishers' NIMAS files.
- The regulations require states and local education agencies to make sure that students who need accessible formats receive them **in a timely manner**, and generally at the same time other students receive their print materials.
- The requirement to produce a NIMAS-compliant file reaches publishers **through the purchasing contracts** of states and school districts. No statutory requirement is placed directly on publishers.
- Eligibility for specialized formats made from NIMAC files is limited. The student must be served under IDEA and must qualify under the definition of "blind or other persons with print disabilities," which comes from the 1931 act commonly called "An Act to Provide Books for the Adult Blind." A student who is served only under Section 504 is not eligible through this route unless they otherwise qualify.
- The regulations also say that students who need accessible formats but cannot get them through NIMAS-derived files must still receive materials in a timely manner by other means.

**Why this matters to a transcriber.** NIMAS files come from a standard, so BrailleBlaster can rely on their structure: headings, lists, page numbers, math, and image descriptions. A transcriber who understands this knows *why* a NIMAS file is more reliable than an arbitrary Word file, and *what to say* when a district says it has no accessible source file.

## Copyright and accessible formats

Copyright law includes provisions that allow authorized entities to make accessible copies for eligible people. The U.S. provision is **17 U.S.C. §121**, often called the Chafee Amendment. Your agency's counsel decides how it applies to your work. A transcriber should know that the question exists, should never assume that "it's for a student" settles it, and should follow the agency's procedures for source files and distribution.

## Professional context

Braille production sits inside several professional pathways. These change over time, so confirm current requirements with each organization before you tell a learner they apply.

- **Teachers of students with visual impairments (TVIs)** are certified or licensed by their states. Many states specify braille competency requirements.
- **Braille transcriber certifications** are offered by organizations including the Library of Congress National Library Service for the Blind and Print Disabled, and the National Federation of the Blind. Check each program for its current exam content and whether it covers software-based transcription.
- **Continuing education** credit may be available for training through your state or through professional organizations. If you plan to offer credit for this course, the roadmap in the repository's `PROFESSIONALIZATION_ROADMAP.md` lists what a provider usually needs to document.
