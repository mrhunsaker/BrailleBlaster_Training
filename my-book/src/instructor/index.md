# Instructor Guide

This guide is for anyone teaching the course to a group, whether you are a university instructor, a district braille coordinator, or a colleague running a workshop.

## What is in this guide

- **This page:** how to run a session, materials, and accommodations.
- **Teaching notes** for [Unit 1](unit-1.md), [Unit 2](unit-2.md), and [Unit 3](unit-3.md): a demonstration idea, tips and pitfalls, a check for understanding, and an extension for each lesson.
- **[Assessment and Grading](assessment.md):** how to use answer keys, unit checkpoints, and the capstone rubric.

## The teaching philosophy

Three ideas run through the whole course. Say them out loud often.

1. **Judgment, not button presses.** The hard part of transcription is deciding what to preserve, what to drop, and what is really structure versus only looking like structure. BrailleBlaster supplies the tools. The learner supplies the decisions.
2. **Choose what the text is.** Styles describe structure. Learners coming from word processors expect "formatting" to mean fonts and colors, so reframe it early.
3. **Check the braille.** Every change should be followed by a look at the Braille View. A correct-looking Print View proves nothing.

## A standard lesson sequence

Most lessons fit this rhythm.

1. **Orient (5 min).** State the goal in one sentence and say why it matters on the job.
2. **Demonstrate (15 to 20 min).** Work the Worked Demonstration live with the Braille View visible.
3. **Guided practice (20 to 30 min).** Do the first practice items together, thinking aloud.
4. **Independent practice.** Learners work the rest alone, then compare with the Answer Key.
5. **Debrief (10 min).** Discuss the items where answers differed. In braille formatting, more than one answer is often defensible if it is applied consistently and explained.

## Preparing to teach

- Install and test BrailleBlaster on the room computers. Match the version your learners will use at work. Remember to uninstall 2.1 before installing 3.x.
- Turn on the toolbars you will use. The Math toolbar is not shown by default, so enable it before the math lessons.
- If you project the screen, increase font size and, in the ASCII Math Hub, expand the Hub to full screen (**Settings > Full Screen** inside the Hub) so learners at the back can follow.
- Have real braille paper on hand for [Lesson 13](../lessons/13-paper-size-and-embossers.md). Physical samples of interpoint and single-sided pages teach more than any description.
- Collect a few *messy* real-world sources: a Word file with fake headings, a Markdown file, a LaTeX file. Clean examples hide the judgment calls this field requires.
- Keep a worked copy of every practice document to compare against.

## Adjusting the course to your group

- **A cohort with Word-processing or desktop-publishing experience** can move through Lessons 1 to 6 quickly and spend longer on Units 2 and 3.
- **A cohort of classroom teachers with no document-production background** should slow down in Unit 1, especially Lessons 2, 4, and 5, and may skip Unit 3.
- **A cohort of experienced transcribers** can sample Unit 1 with the Worked Demonstrations only and spend most time on Lessons 14 to 24 and the capstone.
- **A cohort that will mostly do math** should give extra time to Lessons 7 to 10.

Every lesson stands alone, so you can reorder or split sessions to match your length and your learners. Respect the prerequisites listed on each lesson.

## Supporting learners who use assistive technology

- Ask learners to try [Lesson 1](../lessons/01-navigating-the-interface.md) with their own screen reader or braille display before the course starts, and note any problems.
- Prefer the Answer Keys and descriptions in the text over any visual layout. Practice blocks are plain text for this reason.
- Allow extra time for tasks that rely on switching views.
- Share any accessibility issues you find with the BrailleBlaster project.
