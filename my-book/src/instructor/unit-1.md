# Teaching Notes: Unit 1: Universal Lessons

These notes support Lessons 1 to 13. Teach with BrailleBlaster open and a real, realistically messy source on screen whenever you can.

## Lesson 1: Navigating the BrailleBlaster Interface

**Read the lesson:** [Navigating the BrailleBlaster Interface](../lessons/01-navigating-the-interface.md). **Suggested time:** 60 minutes.

### Check for understanding

- Can name the three views and say where text is edited.
- Can hide, show, and rearrange views and find Breadcrumbs.
- Opens the Braille Preview and explains print versus ordinal page navigation.

## Lesson 2: Opening and Converting Word Documents

**Read the lesson:** [Opening and Converting Word Documents](../lessons/02-opening-word-documents.md). **Suggested time:** 180 minutes.

### Demonstration idea

Bring two versions of the same short document into a training session: one built with genuine Word heading styles and list formatting, and a second, visually identical copy where every "heading" is just bolded, larger, centered text, and every "list" is manually indented with tabs and hyphens. Open both in BrailleBlaster and compare:

- In the first, headings and lists should map to the correct BrailleBlaster styles automatically, and Breadcrumbs will show real heading/list containers.
- In the second, everything should land as Body Text, with the bolding and indentation preserved as visual artifacts rather than structure — a vivid, hands-on demonstration of exactly the gap this lesson is about.

### Tips and pitfalls

**Tip:** This exercise — the same document, built two ways — is one of the most effective demonstrations in the entire curriculum, because it's the one place where "invisible structure" becomes visible through its consequences. Learners who see it once rarely forget it.

A common pitfall for learners moving fast: correcting the *visible* symptom (making a paragraph bold again after it lost its bolding) instead of the *structural* cause (applying the correct heading style, which then handles emphasis appropriately as part of that style). Keep steering learners back to "what is this, structurally?" rather than "how do I make it look right again?"

### Check for understanding

- Predicts, from the Word Style pane, how a file will import, then verifies.
- Corrects styles instead of inserting spaces.
- Keeps the original file and works on a copy.

### Extension activity

Ask learners to take any short Word document they have on hand (or provide one deliberately built with fake structure) and produce a short written diagnosis before opening BrailleBlaster: which elements are they confident are real structure, which look suspicious, and what they'll check for once the file is open. Then have them open it, compare their prediction to what actually happened, and correct any mis-mapped elements.

## Lesson 3: Typeface Emphasis

**Read the lesson:** [Typeface Emphasis](../lessons/03-typeface-emphasis.md). **Suggested time:** 120 minutes.

### Demonstration idea

Give learners this short passage to transcribe by hand as a first exercise:

The captain's log read, *"We are three days from port,"* and beneath it, in the margin, someone had written **do not open until instructed**.

Walk through the decisions out loud:
- The italicized quotation is a genuine print distinction (a direct quote) — apply *Italics*.
- The bolded margin note is print emphasis that should transfer — apply **Bold**.
- If the margin note was also handwritten in the original (a common textbook device for "student annotations"), that's a candidate for *Script* instead of Bold, since Script specifically signals handwriting-like text rather than typographic boldness. This is a good moment to point out that BrailleBlaster gives you the *tool*, but deciding *which* tool matches the print intent is the actual transcription skill.

### Tips and pitfalls

**Tip:** New transcribers often try to apply emphasis and then type — that is, they turn on Bold and expect everything they type next to be bold, the way a word processor works. BrailleBlaster's Emphasis tools work the opposite way: select existing text first, then apply the emphasis. This single habit change is worth drilling before moving on.

**Tip:** Ask learners to open the **Braille View** side-by-side with the **Print View** for this entire chapter. Seeing the emphasis indicators actually appear and disappear in braille as they toggle bold/italics/underline on and off does more to build intuition than any amount of explanation.

A common early mistake is treating every instance of print bold as worth preserving. Encourage learners to ask "would a braille reader lose meaning without this?" — chapter titles restated in bold within running text, decorative bolding, and inconsistent publisher styling are all candidates for being dropped rather than dutifully reproduced.

### Check for understanding

- Chooses symbol, word, or passage indicators correctly, including the two-word case.
- Identifies at least two situations where emphasis should be disregarded.
- Defends the decision to keep or drop a highlight.

### Extension activity

Hand learners a one-page excerpt from any print textbook containing at least: one bolded key term, one italicized title, one underlined phrase, and one instance of highlighting or colored text. Ask them to transcribe the formatting decisions onto a worksheet before touching BrailleBlaster — for each instance, they should note whether they'd preserve it, and if so, with which tool. Then have them apply their decisions in BrailleBlaster and compare the Braille View output against their worksheet.

## Lesson 4: Applying Layout Styles

**Read the lesson:** [Applying Layout Styles](../lessons/04-layout-styles.md). **Suggested time:** 150 minutes.

### Demonstration idea

Take a short print excerpt with a heading, two paragraphs of body text, and a three-item bulleted list:

1. Paste the raw text — it lands entirely in Body Text.
2. Select the heading line, apply the appropriate heading level.
3. Leave the two paragraphs as Body Text (the default is usually already correct).
4. Select the three list lines and apply the List style. Notice, in Breadcrumbs, the CONTAINER label wrapping all three lines as one unit.
5. Use `Ctrl+R` on the second and third list items after applying it to the first, to demonstrate how much faster Repeat Last Style is for uniform material.

### Tips and pitfalls

**Tip:** Learners coming from word processors expect "formatting" to mean fonts and colors. Spend a minute up front reframing styles as *structural* — you're telling BrailleBlaster what kind of thing this text *is* (a heading, a list, a quotation), not how it should merely look. The visual result follows from that identity, per BANA rules, rather than being something you dial in by hand.

**Tip:** The CONTAINER concept in Breadcrumbs is one of the more confusing ideas for new transcribers, because it isn't visible in the Print View at all — it only shows up in Breadcrumbs. Make a habit of keeping Breadcrumbs on during the entire styles unit.

A frequent pitfall: applying a list style to text that isn't actually a structured list in the source (for example, a run of short sentences that merely *look* list-like because they're short). Encourage learners to ask whether the print material is doing something structural (enumeration, sequence, parallel items) before reaching for the List style.

### Check for understanding

- Distinguishes emphasis from styles.
- Reads a Breadcrumbs trail aloud or in writing at three points.
- Uses Repeat Last Style and Unwrap Element correctly.

### Extension activity

Give learners a one-page excerpt containing at least two heading levels, a short numbered list, and a block quotation. Have them apply styles from scratch, then use Breadcrumbs to narrate, out loud or in writing, the full style "trail" at three different points in the document.

## Lesson 7: The Math Translation Toggle

**Read the lesson:** [The Math Translation Toggle](../lessons/07-math-translation-toggle.md). **Suggested time:** 150 minutes.

### Demonstration idea

Take a short paragraph of prose that contains one inline reference to a variable and one displayed equation on its own line:

Solve for *x* in the equation below.
>
x + 5 = 12

1. Select "x" in the first line, toggle Math Translation (`Ctrl+M`), then apply **Nemeth Inline** if the document uses Nemeth for math.
2. Select the displayed equation on its own line, toggle Math Translation, then apply **Nemeth Block**, since it's a self-contained displayed equation rather than a word inside a sentence.
3. Compare the two results in the Braille View — learners should be able to see and describe the difference between the inline indicators (tight to the material, space-separated) and the block indicators (their own line before and after).

### Tips and pitfalls

**Tip:** This is a good moment to explicitly separate two different decisions learners are making at once: (1) *is this math?* (the `Ctrl+M` toggle) and (2) *how should the boundary be marked?* (Block vs. Inline). Learners sometimes reach for Nemeth Block/Inline before flagging the math translation at all — walk through the two-step order every time until it's automatic.

A common pitfall: forgetting that Nemeth Block/Inline only matters when Nemeth material appears *within* a UEB document. If the whole document's translation setting is a Nemeth-based table already, these indicators generally aren't needed, because there's no code-switch for the reader to navigate. This is worth flagging so learners don't apply indicators reflexively regardless of context.

### Check for understanding

- Flags subtraction and multiplication as math but leaves compound-word hyphens alone.
- Chooses Block versus Inline with the sentence test.
- Explains why the toggle cannot be automatic.

### Extension activity

Give learners a short worksheet passage containing three inline variable references and one displayed, multi-term equation. Have them flag each as math, choose Block or Inline appropriately for each instance, and then explain — in their own words — why they made each choice.

## Lesson 8: Complex Equations and the ASCII Math Hub

**Read the lesson:** [Complex Equations and the ASCII Math Hub](../lessons/08-ascii-math-hub.md). **Suggested time:** 180 minutes.

### Demonstration idea

A classic first Hub exercise, and a good one to use explicitly with learners, is **a complex fraction** — a fraction whose numerator or denominator is itself a fraction, which is awkward to type from memory but sits right in the Hub's Examples tab:

1. Place the cursor where the fraction belongs and toggle math translation on if it isn't already.
2. Open **Math > ASCII Math Hub**.
3. Go to the **Examples** tab and locate the complex fraction entry — note both its ASCII Math code and its printed appearance shown side by side.
4. Either insert the example directly and edit the placeholder numbers, or use it as a template to type the equivalent structure with your own values, using the **Search** tab if you need to look up any additional symbol (a variable with a subscript, for instance).
5. Click **Insert**, then check the Braille View to see the resulting Nemeth or UEB Technical rendering of the complex fraction.

This example is worth using precisely because it's the kind of structure a transcriber can't reliably improvise from memory — it's exactly the case the Hub was built for.

### Tips and pitfalls

**Tip:** Some learners try to type ASCII Math directly into the Print View from the very first lesson, skipping the Hub entirely, because they've read about ASCII Math syntax online. That's a legitimate long-term goal — experienced transcribers often do work faster typing directly — but for anyone still building fluency, starting with the Hub (especially the Examples tab) builds correct habits and avoids silent syntax errors that only show up as garbled braille.

A common early mistake is not noticing that entered math is highlighted pink and mistaking a plain, un-flagged equation for a completed one. Make it routine to check the Print View highlighting immediately after every Hub insertion.

**Tip:** The Hub can be expanded to fill the whole screen (**Settings > Full Screen** inside the Hub window), and its font size can be increased or decreased with the Plus/Minus buttons next to the Font Size control. For a training room with a projector, maximizing the Hub before demonstrating a complex symbol search makes it far easier for learners at the back of the room to follow along.

### Check for understanding

- Builds at least six of the ten practice equations in the Hub.
- Uses the Examples tab for composite structures.
- Verifies pink flagging and the Braille View.

### Extension activity

Give learners three print expressions of increasing complexity: a simple fraction, a complex (nested) fraction, and an expression involving a Greek letter and an exponent. Have them build all three using the ASCII Math Hub, insert them, and then locate each one again afterward using Previous Math/Next Math to practice navigating math elements in a longer document.

## Lesson 9: The Spatial Math Editor

**Read the lesson:** [The Spatial Math Editor](../lessons/09-spatial-math-editor.md). **Suggested time:** 180 minutes.

### Demonstration idea

Long division is a natural first Spatial Math Editor exercise because it's instantly recognizable and makes the value of a spatial layout tool obvious even to learners skeptical that "regular" ASCII Math wouldn't be enough:

1. Place the cursor where the problem belongs; open **Math > Spatial Math Editor**.
2. Choose **Math Templates** as the Container Type, then **Division Template** as the Template Type.
3. Set the number of **Operands** the problem requires.
4. If the exercise should show a worked solution rather than just the blank problem, set **Solution** to **True** and enter it; otherwise leave it **False** to produce a blank practice problem for the student to complete.
5. Click **Insert**, then compare the Braille View rendering against a Nemeth long-division reference to confirm the spatial layout translated as expected.

### Tips and pitfalls

**Tip:** Because the Spatial Math Editor is a separate window, learners sometimes go looking for spatial math edits inside the ordinary Print View and conclude the tool is broken when nothing happens. Reinforce, early and often: place the cursor near the spatial math *first*, then open the Editor — the Editor reads what's under the cursor and lets you revise it there, not in the main document view.

A common pitfall with matrices specifically: not anticipating page-width overflow until the document is nearly finished. For matrices with many columns or long cell contents, check the **Wide Overflow Style** setting early, rather than discovering a matrix doesn't fit the braille page only at final proofing.

### Check for understanding

- Chooses the correct container type for each item.
- Does not fake spatial structure with spaces.
- Verifies alignment in both views.

### Extension activity

Provide learners with a printed page containing a 3×3 matrix and a two-operand long-division problem. Have them reproduce both using the Spatial Math Editor, then swap completed files with a partner to proofread each other's Braille View output against the original print page.

## Lesson 10: Spatial Number Lines

**Read the lesson:** [Spatial Number Lines](../lessons/10-spatial-number-lines.md). **Suggested time:** 150 minutes.

### Demonstration idea

Reproduce a simple print number line: a line from 0 to 1, counted by quarters, with a shaded interval (filled circle) from ¼ to ¾:

1. Open the Spatial Math Editor, choose Number Line Editor.
2. Leave **Reduce Fraction** on (this line is meant to show reduced quarters, not an unreduced running denominator).
3. Set **Number Line View** to *Math Entry*.
4. Set **Interval/Points** to *Interval*; set both **Start Interval Type** and **End Interval Type** to *Full Circle*, matching a print line where both endpoints of the shaded segment are solid dots.
5. Set **Count By** to ¼, **Line Start** to 0, **Line End** to 1, **Interval Start** to ¼, and **Interval End** to ¾.
6. Click **Insert**, then compare the Braille View output against the print original, checking specifically that the fraction values, the arrows (if the print original has them), and the interval endpoints all match.

### Tips and pitfalls

**Tip:** Reduce Fraction is the setting most likely to produce a *technically working but pedagogically wrong* result — the braille will translate cleanly either way, but if the print lesson's entire point was showing unreduced equivalent fractions, silently reducing them changes what the material teaches. This is a good moment to remind learners that transcription decisions can affect meaning even when nothing is "broken" in a technical sense.

A frequent early mistake is entering Line Start/Line End/Count By values that aren't mathematically consistent with each other (an increment that doesn't evenly divide the range), which can produce an unexpected or malformed line. If the number line doesn't come out as expected, re-check that the numbers entered actually describe a sensible sequence before assuming a tool problem.

### Check for understanding

- Distinguishes open, closed, and mixed endpoints correctly.
- Chooses Math Entry or User Text with a reason.
- Checks the translation.

### Extension activity

Give learners two print number lines to reproduce: one showing a deliberately unreduced sequence of fractions (to practice recognizing when Reduce Fraction should be turned off), and one using open circles at both ends of a shaded interval (to practice Start/End Interval Type). Have them explain, for each, which settings they chose and why.

## Lesson 11: Using the Contraction Relaxer

**Read the lesson:** [Using the Contraction Relaxer](../lessons/11-contraction-relaxer.md). **Suggested time:** 120 minutes.

### Demonstration idea

A useful, low-stakes way to introduce this tool: take a short passage of standard contracted braille and deliberately relax a single, common contraction (one used often enough in the passage that its effect is obvious). Compare the Braille View before and after, and count how many individual cells the passage now uses compared to fully-contracted output — this makes the trade-off tangible: relaxing even one contraction has a real, measurable cost in braille length, which is worth weighing consciously against the specific benefit for the specific reader.

### Tips and pitfalls

**Tip:** This is a good moment to connect back to [Lesson 3](../lessons/03-typeface-emphasis.md)'s closing point about judgment: the Contraction Relaxer is a tool for making a considered accommodation, not a way to sidestep learning standard contracted braille conventions. Frame it explicitly in terms of *who* the relaxed contractions are serving and *why*, every time it's used.

A common misunderstanding is treating the Contraction Relaxer as equivalent to switching a document to an uncontracted translation profile. They produce different results and serve different purposes — reach for a full uncontracted profile when the entire document should be Grade 1 throughout, and reach for the Contraction Relaxer when only specific contractions, for a specific reason, need to be suppressed while the rest of the document remains fully contracted.

### Check for understanding

- Explains the instructional reason for relaxing a contraction.
- Uses the Relaxer, Ctrl+Shift+T, and Direct translation appropriately.
- Writes a short, reusable record of what was relaxed and why.

### Extension activity

Give learners a paragraph of standard contracted braille output and ask them to identify, in the Braille View, every instance of one specific common contraction. Have them relax that single contraction using the Contraction Relaxer, compare the before-and-after cell counts, and write one sentence describing a realistic scenario where that specific trade-off would be worth making.

## Lesson 12: Global Find and Replace with Formatting

**Read the lesson:** [Global Find and Replace with Formatting](../lessons/12-find-and-replace.md). **Suggested time:** 150 minutes.

### Demonstration idea

Return to the poorly-formatted Word document scenario from [Lesson 2](../lessons/02-opening-word-documents.md), where every fake "heading" was built as bolded, centered, larger-font text rather than a real Heading style, and — after correction — those paragraphs are now properly styled as Heading 1, but many of them are still, individually, also carrying leftover Bold emphasis from the original file:

1. Open **Edit > Find and Replace**, click **Formatting**.
2. Under Styles, select **Heading 1** and click **Add** to the Find condition — leave Find Text blank, so this matches any text within a Heading 1, regardless of content.
3. Under Replace, add **Bold** removed... since Bold can't be directly removed through Replace, instead use the dedicated **Emphasis > Remove All Emphasis from Headings** tool covered in [Lesson 3](../lessons/03-typeface-emphasis.md) for this specific case, and reserve Find and Replace with Formatting for cases where you're changing *which* style or attribute applies, not stripping one outright.
4. As a genuinely global-replace example instead: search for a term that was spelled two different ways throughout an inconsistent source document (for example, both "co-operate" and "cooperate"), with Match Case off, and use Replace All to standardize the spelling throughout — narrating out loud, for learners, why Replace All is safe here (a real, unambiguous spelling correction) versus a case where individually reviewing each Replace would be safer (a term that has different correct meanings in different contexts).

### Tips and pitfalls

**Tip:** **Replace All** is fast and, used carelessly, is exactly how a document ends up with a batch error repeated fifty times instead of corrected once. Teach learners to run **Find** first, scroll through a few results to confirm the pattern really is what they think it is, and only then use Replace or Replace All.

A common pitfall with formatting-based searches: forgetting that only one style or attribute can be added to a condition at a time, and expecting a combined "Heading 1 AND Bold" search to work as one query. Plan formatting corrections as a short sequence of single-condition passes instead.

### Check for understanding

- Predicts which scenario would damage a document under Replace All.
- Uses Whole Word and Match Case deliberately.
- Replaces a style through the Formatting window.

### Extension activity

Give learners a document with a spelling inconsistency repeated at least five times, and a formatting inconsistency (for example, some but not all list-prefix numbers carrying leftover bold emphasis) affecting at least three elements. Have them correct the spelling with a plain Replace All, and the formatting inconsistency using Find and Replace with Formatting, documenting the exact search conditions they used for each.

## Lesson 13: Paper Size and Connecting an Embosser

**Read the lesson:** [Paper Size and Connecting an Embosser](../lessons/13-paper-size-and-embossers.md). **Suggested time:** 180 minutes.

### Demonstration idea

Before a learner embosses anything for the first time, have them walk through the full chain deliberately: set Page Properties for the classroom's actual braille paper stock, create an embosser profile for the room's actual embosser (using Generic/Text with margins if the exact make and model isn't in the list), and then emboss a single short practice page — checking, physically, that the margins on the embossed sheet match what was set on screen.

### Tips and pitfalls

**Tip:** The distinction between Text only and Text with margins trips up more new transcribers than almost anything else in this lesson, because the visible symptom of getting it wrong — margins that are doubled, or missing entirely — looks like a translation problem rather than an embosser-profile problem. If margins look wrong on a physical embossed page but look correct in the Braille Preview, the embosser profile setting is the first thing to check, not the document's styles.

A frequent early mistake is reflexively clicking **Make Default** the first time page properties are set, before an agency's actual house standard has been confirmed — which then silently applies non-standard settings to every future document until someone notices. Encourage learners to use **OK** (current document only) until settings are deliberately finalized.

**Tip:** Frame Advanced embosser options explicitly as a *last resort*, not a first stop. New transcribers troubleshooting a bad emboss sometimes head straight for Advanced options because they look like "the technical settings," when the actual problem is far more often a wrong Manufacturer/Model selection or a physical connection issue. Teach the diagnostic order above as a habit, not just a reference list.

### Check for understanding

- Matches page geometry to paper and sets interpoint.
- Explains why a correct Braille View does not replace a test emboss.
- Troubleshoots in the order: model, generic text, then advanced options.

### Extension activity

Have each learner set up Page Properties for a specific, real braille paper stock provided in class, create an embosser profile for the training room's device, and emboss a one-paragraph test page. Ask them to physically measure the margins on the output with a ruler and compare against the settings they entered.
