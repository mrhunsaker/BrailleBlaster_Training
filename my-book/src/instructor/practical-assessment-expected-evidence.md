# Practical Assessment: Expected Evidence and Evaluator Reference

This is an instructor-only companion to the candidate packet. It describes evidence that should normally appear in a competent submission. It is not a rigid answer key: source formats, BrailleBlaster versions, and valid production decisions may differ. Score the quality of the candidate's reasoning, source fidelity, and verification.

> [!NOTE]
> Treat the evidence examples as indicators of competent reasoning, not a rigid answer key. Accept an alternative result when the candidate demonstrates source fidelity, applicable standards, reader-centered judgment, and independent verification.

## Station 1 — Intake and source assessment

A competent candidate should normally:

- list every supplied source and identify working copies;
- distinguish the authoritative content source from derivative or conversion outputs;
- inspect headings, lists, tables, equations, graphics, encoding, and reading order as applicable;
- identify risks such as flattened structure, missing alt text, ambiguous math, missing table headers, unsupported assumptions, or incomplete metadata;
- request clarification where source evidence is insufficient; and
- make a reasoned proceed, proceed-with-exceptions, or hold recommendation.

**Insufficient evidence:** a file list without provenance; a generic “looks fine” statement; or undocumented guessing when sources conflict.

## Station 2 — Import and semantic repair

A competent candidate should normally:

- choose an import method appropriate to the supplied format and task;
- work on a copy and retain the original;
- use meaningful structural elements rather than visual spacing or font appearance alone;
- preserve heading hierarchy, list nesting, emphasis scope, table relationships, and content order;
- identify losses or transformations introduced by import;
- document repairs and compare the result to the source; and
- save a project that can be reopened for review.

**Insufficient evidence:** a visually plausible result with no source comparison; silent omission of content; or repairs that alter meaning.

## Station 3 — Specialized content decisions

### Mathematics

Expected evidence includes a clear identification of mathematical context and boundaries, source comparison, and verification of operators, signs, grouping, subscripts/superscripts where relevant, and expression order. A candidate should not assume that every numeral or symbol is mathematical, nor that every math-looking sequence can be treated as ordinary prose.

### Tables

Expected evidence includes identification of the table's purpose, row/column relationships, headers, units, sequence, and all data values. If the candidate proposes linearization or another treatment, the rationale should explain how a reader can recover the essential relationships without losing information.

### Figures

Expected evidence includes a decision based on the figure's instructional purpose and information content. A description should communicate essential relationships, labels, trends, or process information without inventing facts. A tactile-graphic brief should identify the intended reader task, essential features, labels, and simplification needs. Description, tactile graphic, combined treatment, or omission may be defensible when justified.

**Critical concern:** a treatment that materially changes mathematical meaning, omits essential table data, or misrepresents a figure.

## Station 4 — Production control and proofreading

A competent candidate should normally provide:

- a coherent pagination and volume plan that treats page counts as estimates until verified;
- consideration of running heads, print/braille page numbering, volume boundaries, contents placement, and relevant preliminary pages;
- defect records with location, category/severity, source evidence, correction, and verification;
- evidence that corrections were checked in surrounding context; and
- a release recommendation that holds the work when a critical issue remains unresolved.

**Insufficient evidence:** a pagination plan with no actual verification step; a list of corrections without source evidence; or a “complete” status despite a known critical defect.

## Station 5 — Handoff and oral defense

A competent handoff should identify deliverable filenames, source/version provenance, project state, completed quality checks, limitations, assumptions, unresolved questions, and next actions. Oral explanations should point to evidence in the artifacts.

## Cross-station critical-defect indicators

Evaluate the consequence, not just the presence of a technical imperfection. A critical defect may include:

- material content loss or substitution without authorization;
- materially incorrect or unverified mathematical content;
- missing essential table data or broken relationships;
- figure treatment that misleads the reader about essential information;
- inability to establish which source/version was used; or
- a serious known proofreading regression in the proposed release.

Document the affected artifact and location, the reader or production impact, what the candidate knew, and the candidate's release decision. Apply the critical-defect override in the score sheet.

## Acceptable variation

Do not penalize a candidate merely because their workflow differs from the evaluator's preferred sequence. Accept alternatives that meet the task, preserve content, are supported by source evidence, are documented, and can be verified. Escalation is appropriate when the source does not support a safe decision.

## Evidence bundle checklist

- [ ] Source inventory and provenance record
- [ ] Risk register and clarification log
- [ ] Working source and repaired source
- [ ] BrailleBlaster project saved and reopenable
- [ ] Source-to-output comparison
- [ ] Math/table/figure decision evidence
- [ ] Pagination and volume plan
- [ ] Defect log and verification evidence
- [ ] Handoff note
- [ ] Oral prompt response record
- [ ] Completed observation checklist and score sheet
