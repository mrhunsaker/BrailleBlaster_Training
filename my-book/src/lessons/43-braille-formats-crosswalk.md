## Learning objectives

By the end of this lesson you will be able to:

- Use *Braille Formats 2016* to find the rule behind a formatting decision.
- Say which authority governs which question: the code books, Braille Formats, the agency, or the student's team.
- Map each course lesson to the sections of Braille Formats and the code books it relies on.
- Read a Braille Formats rule: its number, its sub-rules, and its Braille Reader's Perspective notes.

> [!IMPORTANT]
> A crosswalk helps locate authority; it does not replace the current code books, applicable Braille Formats edition, agency policy, or the student's documented requirements. Check the edition and jurisdiction before treating a cited rule as controlling.

## Why this matters

Every lesson in this course says *what BrailleBlaster does*. A transcriber also needs to know **why**, and to be able to defend a choice to a teacher, a reviewer, or a student's team. The reason is almost always in one of a small number of documents. This lesson is the map: which document answers which question, and where in the course each one is used.

## The Concept

### Who decides what

When two sources seem to disagree, ask which one governs the question.

| Question | Who decides | Examples |
| -------- | ----------- | -------- |
| **Which symbol is used?** | The **code book**: *The Rules of Unified English Braille* for literary text, and the *Nemeth Code* for math | Contractions, indicators, a math symbol |
| **How is it laid out on the page?** | ***Braille Formats: Principles of Print-to-Braille Transcription, 2016*** | Headings, margins, lists, page numbers, tables, special pages |
| **What does this agency do when the rules allow a choice?** | The **agency style sheet** ([Lesson 48](48-job-intake-and-the-agency-style-sheet.md)) | The heading map, the wording of a Transcriber's Note |
| **What does this reader need?** | **The student's team**: teacher, TVI, and family | A different code, uncontracted braille, extra description |
| **What should I do when none of these answers it?** | **The transcriber's judgment**, written down | A Transcriber's Note, and a line in the production log |

The first three are written down and are the same for everyone. The fourth is specific to a student. The fifth is a last resort, and the log is what protects it.

### How a Braille Formats rule is built

The standard is organized like a legal text. It uses decimal numbers: **§4** is the chapter on headings, **§4.2** a section of it, **§4.2.1** a rule within the section. A rule may have lettered sub-rules, such as **§8.5.1(b)**.

Three kinds of text help you read it:

- **The rule itself.** Stated in a sentence, often with "shall" or "may". The difference matters: *shall* is required, and *may* is a choice.
- **An example.** Worked cases, which are usually the quickest way to see what a rule means.
- **A Braille Reader's Perspective note.** An explanation of *why* a rule exists, from the reader's side. When a rule seems arbitrary, read the note.

## Step by step

### Look up a rule

1. **Name the question.** Is it a symbol question (code book), a layout question (Formats), or a choice (style sheet)?
2. **Find the chapter** in the table of contents, then the section.
3. **Read the rule, its sub-rules, and the example.** Do not stop at the first sentence.
4. **Read the Braille Reader's Perspective note**, if there is one.
5. **Check whether the rule says *shall* or *may*.**
6. **Write the section number** beside your decision in the production log ([Lesson 54](54-a-model-job-capstone-walkthrough.md)).

### Record a decision you made on judgment

1. State the situation in one sentence.
2. Name the rules you considered.
3. State what you did and why.
4. If it affects the reader, put a **Transcriber's Note** in the braille.

## Practice

### Tasks

Use the crosswalk and, if you have it, your copy of the standard.

1. Which section tells you to compress a print hierarchy of more than three levels?
2. Where do you look to decide whether to keep the bold type on a word in a heading?
3. Which section gives the margins for a simple list, and what are they?
4. Which standard decides which cell stands for a particular math sign: Braille Formats, or the Nemeth Code?
5. Which standard says how many blank lines go before a centered heading?
6. Your agency's style sheet says to use Cell 5 headings for every second-level print heading. A student's team asks for a different treatment. Who decides?
7. A rule in the standard says a transcriber *may* retain emphasis. What does that mean for your decision?
8. Find the section number in your own copy for the table formats, and add it to the table above.

### Answer Key

1. **§4.2.1**, in the section on heading hierarchy.
2. **§5.3.1** (when emphasis may be ignored), together with **§4.3.7** (emphasis in headings). Emphasis on *part* of a heading may need to be kept for distinction.
3. **§8.3.1**. A simple list uses margins **1-3**: the first line starts in cell 1, and any runover starts in cell 3.
4. The **Nemeth Code** decides the symbol. Braille Formats decides how a math expression sits on the page.
5. **Braille Formats**, in the centered-heading section (**§4.4.1**: a centered heading is preceded and followed by a blank line).
6. The **student's team**, within what the standard allows. The style sheet is the default for the agency, and the student's needs take priority. Record the exception in the production log.
7. It is **your judgment**. *May* is not a requirement. Decide from the reader's point of view, and record why.
8. Your answer depends on your copy. Add it to the "where to look" table, and record the edition.

## Key takeaways

- **Symbols** come from the code books. **Layout** comes from *Braille Formats 2016*. **Choices** come from the agency style sheet. **The reader's needs** come from the student's team. **Judgment** is written down.
- A Braille Formats rule has a **number**, sub-rules, **examples**, and often a **Braille Reader's Perspective** note that explains the reason.
- *Shall* is required. *May* is a choice.
- The course already cites sections for scope, headings, typeforms, and lists. Add the rest from your own copy.
